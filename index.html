Computational neuro-oncology · ODU Vision Lab

Trustworthy AI for recurrent brain tumor diagnosis and prognosis.

A model that cannot say "I don't know" should not be making a treatment decision.

I build machine learning for multimodal MRI that separates tumor recurrence from radiation necrosis, predicts survival under censored follow-up, and reports where its own prediction is unreliable. Medical imaging models are evaluated on how often they are right; in a clinic, what matters at least as much is whether they can tell you when they are likely to be wrong.

That work sits inside a wider NIH-funded effort on trustworthy AI for high-grade brain tumor recurrence. My dissertation pushes it one level down, asking which conclusions are recoverable at all from clinical data this sparse and this noisy.

01

The problem I work on

Framing

Neuro-oncology is where machine learning's assumptions break all at once.

Scans are noisy and vary by scanner and protocol. Survival outcomes are censored when patients are lost to follow-up. Cohorts run to 50 or 200 patients, not 50,000, and the classes that matter clinically are the rare ones. Meanwhile the decisions cannot wait: is this enhancement recurrence or radiation necrosis, how long does this patient have, does this treatment plan need to change.

Most methods are built for pristine data and degrade quietly when they meet this. Quietly is the dangerous part. A model that is 8% less accurate and says so is usable. A model that is 8% less accurate and reports the same confidence as before is not.

So the research question is not only how to make these models more accurate. It is how to make them honest about their own limits, and how to know which limits are fundamental to the data rather than fixable with a better architecture.

2021-2024 · NIH / NIBIB · Statistical modeling

Recurrence or radiation necrosis

Two conditions that look the same on MRI and call for opposite decisions.

I extract multiresolution radiomic and fractal texture features to separate them, then pair that with a copula-based survival model. The copula matters because standard Cox regression assumes censoring is independent of outcome, and in glioblastoma follow-up it is not: the patients who disappear from a study are not a random sample of the patients in it. Modelling that dependence is what makes the prognostic index trustworthy rather than merely fitted.

2021-2023 · AMOS challenge · Uncertainty

Segmentation that reports its own doubt

Which of these outputs should a human look at again?

An nnU-Net ensemble trained with 5-fold cross-validation yields two signals that mean different things: entropy-based total uncertainty, and mutual-information epistemic uncertainty. The separation is the point. Aleatoric uncertainty tells you the image is ambiguous; epistemic uncertainty tells you the model is out of its depth. Only the second is fixable with more data, and only the second should trigger review. Our team placed eighth in AMOS at MICCAI 2022.

2018-present · NIH / NIBIB · MICCAI BraTS

Continuous depth, smaller model

Can a segmentation network shrink enough to run where the patients are?

UNCE-NODE replaces a U-Net's discrete residual stack with a neural ordinary differential equation, sharing weights across integration steps. Roughly 30% fewer trainable parameters at comparable Dice. The motivation is not elegance. Hospitals outside major research centers do not have research clusters, and a method that only runs on one is a method that only helps the patients already best served. The framework has gone into the MICCAI BraTS challenge every year since 2021.

2021-2025 · NIH · Statistical modeling

Function, not just structure

Which functional networks carry the signal in opioid use disorder?

Discrete wavelet features from resting-state fMRI BOLD signals, LASSO selection, random forest classification. The classification result matters less than which networks the model leans on, because that is the part a clinician or neuroscientist can argue with. Interpretability was the design constraint from the start rather than a post-hoc addition.

2023-2026 · Domain shift

When the target population is scarce

What survives transfer from adults to children?

Pediatric glioma datasets are orders of magnitude smaller than adult ones, and pediatric tumors differ in location, anatomy, and enhancement. MF-DNN combines multiresolution fractal descriptors at the encoder bottleneck with gradient-reversal domain alignment, pushing the network toward features an adult-versus-pediatric classifier cannot separate. Monte-Carlo dropout then flags the voxels where that transfer failed anyway, which is the honest part.

2025-present · Dissertation · Physics-based modeling

What is learnable at all

Which tumor growth dynamics can imaging this sparse actually resolve?

PDE-based growth models are limited less by their physics than by how sparsely and noisily we sample the patient. I am developing a Resolvable Expressive Capacity framework with eigentask decomposition and a Bayesian inference layer, to characterize which growth directions are identifiable from a given observation schedule and which are not. The output is a digital twin that reports what it cannot know, which is the same commitment as the uncertainty work, pushed down to the level of the physics.

For prospective students

Mentoring

I have mentored two NSF-REU undergraduates and several Vision Lab researchers. They came from computer science, from electrical engineering with almost no computational background, and from institutions with no medical imaging exposure at all. Every one of them shipped something in ten weeks, which taught me that the binding constraint is rarely talent and almost always structure.

So: weekly milestones, reproducible practice from week one, HPC access early rather than as a reward, and explicit instruction in the things usually left implicit. How to read a paper for its method rather than its claims. When a negative result is worth writing up. How to ask for help before a week is gone.

If you are considering this area, you do not need a medical background. You need to be comfortable being wrong in public, and interested in why a model failed rather than only in whether it did.

Questions I would hand a new studentDoes an uncertainty estimate change what a clinician actually does, or does it just correlate with error? Nobody has measured this properly.
When a model transfers badly to a new hospital, can you tell in advance from the images alone, before you have any labels?
How few scans can you take and still identify a patient's tumor growth parameters? There should be a bound, and it should be computable.
What I look forPersistence with debugging, willingness to read outside your field, and the habit of writing down what you tried. Not prior publications.
